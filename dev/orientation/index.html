Choosing an approach

This page maps common biological and scientific questions to the right causal framework, package, and CausalDynamics API. It assumes familiarity with experimental or observational data analysis (such as regression or mixed models), not prior training in causal inference.

Where this package fits

Causal analysis separates four jobs that are often conflated in standard statistical modelling:

TaskTypical questionPackage
Draw assumptionsHow do variables in my system influence one another?DAGMakie.jl
Identify & simulateWhich covariates must I adjust for (or exclude)? Can this effect be identified at all? How does the system respond to an intervention?CausalDynamics.jl (this package)
Estimate effectsGiven data and an adjustment set, what is the effect of shifting an exposure or treatment?CausalTargeted.jl
Decompose pathwaysHow much of an effect acts directly versus through an intermediate mechanism?CausalMediation.jl

A standard workflow moves from left to right: state the causal graph, obtain an identification certificate from CausalDynamics, and pass that certificate to an estimator in CausalTargeted or CausalMediation.

Why draw a causal graph before fitting a model?

Regression and machine-learning models estimate associations in observed data. A causal question asks something different: how would an outcome $Y$ change if we intervened on an exposure $A$ (for instance, reducing parasite load or altering diet)?

Adding every measured variable to a regression does not fix bias. Whether a covariate helps or harms depends on its causal position relative to exposure $A$ and outcome $Y$ (Pearl, 2009; Cinelli, Forney & Pearl, 2024; Hernán & Robins, 2020):

StructureGraphBiological exampleFor the total effect of $A$ on $Y$
Confounder (fork)$A \leftarrow W \rightarrow Y$Age or season ($W$) affects both parasite load ($A$) and survival ($Y$).Adjust for $W$. Omitting it leaves a spurious non-causal association.
Mediator (chain)$A \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y$Infection ($A$) triggers an immune response ($M$) that affects survival ($Y$).Do not adjust for $M$. Conditioning on $M$ blocks the pathway you want to measure.
Collider$A \rightarrow C \leftarrow Y$Both infection ($A$) and poor condition ($Y$) make an animal easier to trap ($C$).Do not condition on $C$. Restricting to or adjusting for $C$ creates a spurious association between $A$ and $Y$.

CausalDynamics encodes these graphical rules algorithmically (Shpitser, VanderWeele & Robins, 2010; van der Zander, Liśkiewicz & Textor, 2019): you supply the directed acyclic graph (DAG) representing your scientific assumptions, and the package determines whether the target effect is identifiable and which variables belong in the adjustment set. Full citations and DOIs appear in References.

A biological example: good and bad controls

Consider a field study of how parasite load (A) affects overwinter survival (Y):

  • Season and host age (W) influence parasite load, immune response, and survival (a confounder).
  • Parasite load alters an immune marker (M), which in turn affects survival (a mediator).
  • Both heavy parasite load and poor survival prospects affect whether an animal enters a live trap (C, a collider).
using CausalDynamics, Graphs

# Nodes: 1=W (age/season), 2=A (parasite load), 3=M (immune marker),
#        4=Y (survival),   5=C (trap capture)
g = DiGraph(5)
add_edge!(g, 1, 2); add_edge!(g, 1, 3); add_edge!(g, 1, 4)  # W → A, M, Y
add_edge!(g, 2, 3); add_edge!(g, 2, 4)                      # A → M, Y
add_edge!(g, 3, 4)                                          # M → Y
add_edge!(g, 2, 5); add_edge!(g, 4, 5)                      # A → C ← Y (collider)

names = Dict(1 => :W, 2 => :A, 3 => :M, 4 => :Y, 5 => :C)

# 1. Total effect of parasite load (A) on survival (Y)
id_total = identify(g, TotalEffectQuery(:A, :Y); node_names = names)
(identifiable = id_total.identifiable, strategy = id_total.strategy, adjust = id_total.adjustment)
(identifiable = true, strategy = :backdoor, adjust = [:W])

identify selects [:W] and excludes both the mediator :M and the collider :C. If the scientific question shifts to how much of the effect of parasite load operates through the immune marker :M, switch the query to MediationQuery:

# 2. Direct vs immune-mediated effect of A on Y
id_med = identify(
    g,
    MediationQuery(:A, :Y, [:M]; effect_kind = :interventional);
    node_names = names,
)
(identifiable = id_med.identifiable, adjust = id_med.adjustment, mediators = id_med.mediators, moc = id_med.moc)
(identifiable = true, adjust = [:W], mediators = [:M], moc = Symbol[])

The returned IdentificationResult is a structured certificate: it records the graph fingerprint, the identification strategy, the adjustment set, and any exposure-induced mediator–outcome confounders (moc). Passing this object to CausalTargeted or CausalMediation keeps the assumptions explicit and prevents accidental adjustment for mediators or colliders.

What identification can and cannot tell you

  • Identification is prior to data. It asks: if the graph is structurally correct and we had unlimited data, could this causal effect be expressed from the observed distribution? It does not test whether your data are large enough or whether a particular regression model is well specified.
  • Garbage in, garbage out. A valid certificate guarantees only that the adjustment set is sound under the assumed graph. Omitting an unmeasured confounder from the graph will produce an overconfident adjustment set; use omitted-confounder sensitivity analysis in CausalTargeted (Cinelli & Hazlett, 2020) to quantify how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to overturn a conclusion.
  • Not identifiable is a useful answer. When id.identifiable == false, the package is telling you that no statistical adjustment of the observed variables can isolate the causal effect under your graph (Shpitser & Pearl, 2006): you need an instrument, a frontdoor mechanism, longitudinal measurements, a randomised intervention, or a different estimand.

Plain-language glossary

TermPlain meaning
EstimandThe exact causal quantity you want to know (for example, the difference in mean survival if parasite load were reduced by one unit across the population), stated before choosing a statistical estimator (Hernán & Robins, 2020).
Identification vs estimationIdentification decides whether the estimand can be recovered from observed variables under a causal graph; estimation computes a numerical estimate and uncertainty interval from finite data (Pearl, 2009).
Backdoor pathA non-causal path between exposure $A$ and outcome $Y$ that starts with an arrow pointing into $A$ (such as $A \leftarrow W \rightarrow Y$). Backdoor adjustment blocks all such paths without blocking causal paths or opening colliders (Pearl, 2009; Cinelli et al., 2024).
d-separationThe graphical rule that determines whether two variables are statistically independent once you condition on a given set of covariates (Pearl, 2009).
Intervention do(·)Setting a variable to a value or rule externally, breaking the natural arrows that normally determine it (Pearl, 2009).
CounterfactualWhat would have happened to the same individual or trajectory under a different intervention, holding its unobserved background factors ($U$) constant (Pearl, 2009).
Intermediate confounder (moc)A variable affected by the exposure that then confounds the mediator and the outcome. Common in biology (for example, infection alters body condition, which affects both immunity and survival); it requires interventional rather than natural mediation estimands (Avin, Shpitser & Pearl, 2005; VanderWeele, 2015).
